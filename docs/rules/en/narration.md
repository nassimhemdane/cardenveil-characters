# Narrative System

# 🎭 **Narrative System**

---

## **1. Character Structure**

Each character has several **narrative aspects** representing their personality and experiences.

These aspects do not provide fixed bonuses; they influence rolls according to the situation.

### **General Aspects**

- **Background**: your past, profession or upbringing.
    
    *Example: former soldier, street thief, apprentice mage.*
    
- **Goal**: your personal objective or main motivation.
    
    *Example: find my sister, redeem myself, become a master craftsperson.*
    
- **Bonds**: your relationships with other characters, positive or negative.
    
    *Example: I protect Thalen at all costs; I distrust Lyra.*

---

### **Attribute Aspects**

Each attribute (Strength, Agility, Spirit, Social) has **two possible aspects**.

The player chooses one per attribute.

| Attribute | Aspect | Description | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Strength** | **Life Scar** | An ordeal, wound or memory that left its mark on you. | “I survived the mine collapse.” |
|  | **Drive** | The energy or impulse that pushes you to act. | “I refuse to give up, whatever the cost.” |
| **Agility** | **Habits & Tics** | Instinctive physical habits or reflexes. | “I always tap my foot before acting.” |
|  | **Instinct** | A quick, intuitive response, often before thought. | “I always leap before I think.” |
| **Spirit** | **Beliefs** | Your principles, faith and view of the world. | “I believe everything has a hidden reason.” |
|  | **Education** | Your learned knowledge, reasoning and cultural background. | “Trained at Escalune's engineering academy.” |
| **Social** | **Personality** | Your temperament and natural way of being. | “Always sarcastic, even when afraid.” |
|  | **Reputation** | How others see you. | “People think I am braver than I really am.” |

Aspects must be consistent with the character's attributes.

A character with low Social cannot have a perfect reputation.

This is not a numerical rule, but a **consistency requirement**.

> Strong attributes produce aspects the character has mastered.
>
> Weak attributes produce unstable or fragile aspects.

## **2. Aspects in Play**

**Aspects** influence your rolls and situations according to your personality, experiences or behavior.

They can help you or make life harder, depending on the situation.

### **Positive Use**

When your aspect helps you perform an action:

1. Choose a card from your hand and **give it to the GM**.
2. Add to your contextual roll:
    - **Half its value** if the card is **red**.
    - **Its full value** if the card is **black**.
3. The effect is immediate, but the card goes into the GM's hand to be used again later.

Example:

> Aspect: Education — “Trained at Escalune's engineering academy.”
>
> You want to repair a complex mechanism → you give the GM an 8♦.
>
> Add +4 to your roll; the GM keeps the card to use later.

---

### **Negative Use**

Either you or the GM can invoke an aspect against you.

You then draw a **crystallized** card:

- You can choose to **reduce your roll** by the drawn value.
- Or let the GM **create a problem related to your aspect**, whose severity depends on that value.

Example:

> Aspect: Drive — “I charge ahead without thinking.”
>
> You draw a 7.
>
> → Either your roll decreases by 7, or the GM introduces a setback (you stumble, trigger a trap…).

---

## **4. Goals and Roleplaying**

The **Goal** represents your character's personal objective: what they seek to accomplish, repair or attain.

It is an **individual narrative motivation** that guides their choices and priorities.

**Roleplaying** means portraying your character consistently with their personality, values, emotions, flaws and history, even when doing so is not the most advantageous choice.

When you **portray your character consistently**, whether by pursuing their **Goal** or simply staying true to their personality, the GM may reward you with a **random crystallized card**.

> The GM decides whether your actions justify this reward.
>
> The aim is to encourage characterization and narrative decisions, even when they go against the character's immediate interests.

**Example Goals:**

- *Find a missing relative.*
- *Earn my former mentor's forgiveness.*
- *Prevent an old mistake from happening again.*
- *Found a new order or a stable community.*
- *Make amends for my past wrongs.*

A Goal must be **clear, concrete and able to evolve**: it can change throughout the campaign as the character progresses, fails or redefines what they really want.

## **4. Flashbacks**

**Flashbacks** are player initiatives that **change the scenario's reality** or **introduce a past action** that explains a present advantage.

They establish that an event has already happened, preparations were made, or a memory changes the current situation.

Flashback costs are **high**, because they directly alter the narrative.

---

### **Triggering a Flashback**

1. **Announce the flashback** you want to introduce: what your character did or prepared in the past.
2. The GM **sets a target cost**, usually fairly high (generally between 40 and 150, depending on the scale of the change).
    
    > A small detail or a forgotten precaution costs little.
    >
    > A major rewrite or a complete reversal costs a great deal.
    
3. **Pay this cost** with cards:
    - You can use your **crystallized cards**.
    - If the flashback is **related to one of your aspects**, you can also use your **normal cards** (red or black).
    - Your allies can help by adding their **crystallized cards** to the total.
4. If the sum of the values meets or exceeds the set cost, the flashback is accepted and becomes part of the narrative's reality.

---

### **Example**

> Aspect: Education — “Trained at Escalune's engineering academy.”
>
> The generator room is on fire, and the exit appears blocked.
>
> The player declares:
>
> “Actually, when I first saw the reactor plans, I noticed a maintenance hatch that nobody uses.”
>
> The GM sets a **cost of 35**.
>
> The player spends 2 crystallized cards (values 7 and 6) and 3 cards (8, 5, 10, related to their Education aspect).
>
> The total is 36, so the flashback succeeds.
>
> The GM describes: “Indeed, you remember the hatch and manage to open it; smoke rushes through, revealing an escape route.”

## **5. Overall Operation and Philosophy**

---

### **The Complete Play Cycle**

1. The **GM describes** a situation or a roll with something at stake.
2. **Players can invoke** their **positive Aspects** to strengthen themselves (discard → bonus).
3. The **GM or players** can invoke **negative Aspects** to create complications (penalty or twist → crystallized card).
4. **Bonds** determine who can intervene in another player's roll (to help or hinder) and maintain a balance between these two kinds of interaction.
5. **Players** can use their **normal** or **crystallized** cards for **flashbacks**.

---

### **Making a Skill Check with Aspects**

1. 🎲 **Roll the Die**
   - Roll **1d20**.

2. 🧮 **Add Modifiers**
   - Add the **associated attribute modifier**.

3. 🃏 **Apply Aspects (Cards)**
   - **Positive use**: add the card's value.
   - **Negative use**: subtract the card's value.

4. 🧾 **Determine the Result**
   - **Total = kept d20 + modifier + card adjustment**.
   - **Total ≥ DC**: success. Otherwise: failure.

### **System Philosophy**

- **Aspects** define *how the character acts, reacts and sees the world*, rather than their numerical performance.
- Every use of an aspect must be **justified in the narrative**: roleplaying comes before mechanics.
- **Crystallized cards** reward moments when flaws, mistakes or tensions move the story forward.
- The whole system follows a **natural cycle**:
    
    > players invoke their strengths → create risk → gain resources → shape the story.
